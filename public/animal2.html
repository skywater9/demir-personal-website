The common
raccoon

Procyon lotor

The common raccoon is a sturdy, gray-brown mammal with a black face mask, nimble hands, and a ringed tail. As an adaptable omnivore, it helps shape food webs by eating both plants and animals—and is famous for its dexterity, curiosity, and ability to thrive near people.

AN ICONIC NORTH AMERICAN NEIGHBOR

01 THE ANIMAL

Built for a life of discovery.

A closer look at the raccoon's body, senses, and practical talents.

01

Mammal

A warm-blooded vertebrate with fur; females give birth to live young and nurse them with milk. Raccoons belong to the procyonid family, alongside coatis and kinkajous.

02

Weight

Usually about 5–9 kg (11–20 lb), though body size and seasonal weight vary widely. Raccoons in colder regions can be substantially heavier.

03

Height & build

About 23–30 cm (9–12 in) at the shoulder. Head and body length is commonly 40–70 cm (16–28 in), with a bushy, ringed tail adding another 20–40 cm (8–16 in).

04

Speed

They are steady walkers, but can sprint up to about 24 km/h (15 mph) over short distances when startled or in a hurry.

05

What it eats

An opportunistic omnivore: fruits, nuts, seeds, insects, crayfish, frogs, fish, eggs, small animals, and carrion. Its menu shifts with the season and local food supply.

06

Warm, dense fur

The coat has long, coarse outer guard hairs over a thick insulating underfur. In many descriptions, underfur makes up close to 90% of the coat, helping retain warmth in cold and wet conditions.

07

Climber & swimmer

Excellent climbers with sharp, curved claws and flexible ankles; they can descend a tree headfirst. Raccoons also swim well and may forage along shores and in wetlands.

08

Remarkable hands & senses

Five long, sensitive digits let raccoons feel and manipulate objects with precision. Touch is especially important when foraging; keen hearing and night vision help them move after dark.

✳

The famous “washing” Raccoons often turn food over and feel it in water. This handling behavior seems to heighten the sense of touch; it is not simply an attempt to clean a meal.

02 RANGE & HOME

Where wild meets adaptable.

From southern Canada through much of the United States and into Central America, the common raccoon occupies an enormous range.

It favors places with dependable water and shelter: deciduous woods, wetlands, river corridors, coastal marshes, and mixed farmland. Hollow trees make excellent dens, but rock crevices, abandoned burrows, and sheltered structures can serve too.

Raccoons have also become familiar in towns and cities. They use parks, wooded corridors, and buildings for cover, while finding food in many of the same places people live. They are mostly active at night, especially where human activity is high.

A flexible diet and a talent for finding shelter help them live across very different landscapes.

03 MATING & FAMILY

A solitary life, a devoted start.

Raccoons generally forage alone, but their mating season brings them together. Timing varies across their range; in much of the north, mating happens in late winter. Males may travel widely, while females select a sheltered den for raising young.

After roughly 63 days of pregnancy, a female gives birth to a litter, commonly two to five kits. The kits are born blind and helpless. Their mother nurses and protects them in a tree hollow or another secure den, then leads them out as they grow.

Young raccoons learn to climb and forage alongside their mother through spring and summer. They often disperse in autumn, though young females may remain closer to the maternal range. Males do not help raise the litter.

04 A DEEPER PAST

A lineage shaped in the Americas.

Raccoons belong to Procyonidae, a family of New World mammals that also includes coatis, kinkajous, and olingos. The family's fossil record reaches back millions of years in North America. Fossils assigned to the genus Procyon appear in North America by the Pliocene, and the modern common raccoon is known from Pleistocene deposits.

Over time, Procyon lotor became an adaptable species across a wide range of habitats. Its dexterous forepaws, flexible diet, and ability to use natural and human-made shelter are part of why it remains one of the continent's most recognizable wild mammals.
